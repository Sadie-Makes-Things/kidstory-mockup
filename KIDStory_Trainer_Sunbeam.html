BEFORE YOU BEGIN (The Leader's Posture)

  • Facilitation over Performance: This workshop is about shifting the leader's identity from "expert" to "co-learner". That goes for you too!
  • Connection Before Content: This initial activity, even before the official start time, creates an environment where participants engage with one another. It reinforces the concept of contribution and not just consumption. Everyone will have an opportunity to share something personal to whatever degree they want, it relates to what the session is about and it is FUN!
  • The Power of the Pause: Emphasize the "Listen" section. Silence isn't empty; it's the space where the Holy Spirit speaks.

Guidelines for Effective Workshop Facilitation

Your role is to guide the journey, ensuring the environment remains focused on the Word and the work of the Spirit.

  • Prayer-First Preparation: Be still before the Lord to hear the whisperings of the Holy Spirit. The success of a session depends on the Spirit working through you, not your individual performance.
  • The Co-Learner Mindset: You don't need all the answers. Enter every session believing you will learn from the participants as much as they will learn from you.
  • Create a Safe Environment: Learning requires safety.
  • Physically: Ensure the room layout and temperature are comfortable. Arrange the seating in such a way that it lends itself to participation and not lecture.
  • Mentally: Use affirmations and strictly avoid sarcasm or put-downs.
  • Spiritually: Trust that the Holy Spirit can bring revelation to anyone, regardless of how long they have followed Christ.
  • Expect God Encounters: Go into the workshop expecting God to intersect His Story with the lives of everyone present. Move away from "information dumping" and toward "encounter facilitation".
  • Vulnerability & Transparency: When appropriate, be open about your own life. This models honesty and helps the group connect on a deeper level.
  • The Three-Way Listen:
  • Listen to the Holy Spirit: Be ready to delve deeper when the Spirit prompts you to ask more specific questions.
  • Listen to Participants: Listen to the heart of what people are communicating rather than rushing to give your own advice.
  • Listen to Yourself: Quiet the "expert" chatter in your own head to stay in tune with the group's needs.

Jesus Blesses the Children (Connection Before Content)

Opening:

Have the participants get in pairs and share a time when they did not feel welcomed or loved as a child … or when they welcomed someone … or similar.

Go through the Participant Guidelines highlighting how you want people to use their imagination and pretend that they are there when the story is taking place. Who and what do they see? How are people in the story responding to what is happening? Do they smell anything? Do they feel anything? Also, your desire is for everyone to participate. Everyone has a unique perspective to the story which makes it come alive; we will learn from one another as we share our perspectives. Let’s stick to today’s story and not bring in any other Bible stories we may know. For right now, just listen and imagine – you won’t need to follow along in your Bible. So if you brought one, you can close it now.

Background:

We see Jesus interacting with all kinds of people, young and old alike. He meets with people who are very well liked and with some whom most people don’t like at all. Jesus picked some men to follow Him. They were called His disciples. They were learning to live life just like Him. Listen to this story and hear how Jesus and the disciples treat a certain group of people. This story comes from the Bible and so we know it is true. (You can now close your Bible and place it to the side while you tell the story.)

Tell the Story: Mark 10:13-16

One day some parents brought their children to Jesus so he could touch and bless them. But the disciples scolded the parents for bothering him.

When Jesus saw what was happening, he was angry with his disciples. “Let the children come to me. Don’t stop them! For the Kingdom of God belongs to those who are like these children. I tell you the truth, anyone who doesn’t receive the Kingdom of God like a child will never enter it.” Then he gathered the children into his arms and placed his hands on their heads and blessed them.

Retell:

Rope Game: Take a 5-yard piece of rope and tie it together to make a circle. Have the kids grab onto the rope. At your command, the kids move the rope clockwise and when you say, “Stop,” whoever has the knot (or is closest to the knot) has to tell what happened next in the story.

Discover Questions:

  • What did you notice as this story was being told? What feelings did it generate?
  • What did you like about the story?
  • Can you see the children in the story? What expressions are on their faces? How do you think they felt being with the disciples? With Jesus?
  • I wonder what the parents were wanting from Jesus by bringing their children to Him?
  • Imagine bringing one of the "rowdy" children in your group to Jesus. What does Jesus want to bless them with?
  • Imagine you are coming to Jesus. What does He want to bless you with?
  • What does this story tell us about God/Jesus?
  • In what ways do other people in your community act like the disciples towards
  • children? How does the church treat them this way?
  • In what ways do our churches stop children from coming to Jesus?
  • How do I stop children from coming to Jesus?

Response Questions:

  • What does this story tell you about God’s view of children?
  • What is God calling you to because of what you have seen and heard today?
  • What do you sense the Holy Spirit is asking of you because of what you have learned from the story and from our discussion?

The KIDStory Lesson Format

To keep our sessions consistent and effective, we follow a simple five-part rhythm:

Opening: This opening time allows for engagement and sharing of personal stories. Use an activity or question, not directly tied to the main story, but leading in that direction. For example, have participants turn to a neighbor and share a childhood experience of not feeling welcomed or loved. Following this is the...

Background: The pre-story statement prepares everyone for "God's Story." Does your preparation include essential background information, a transition from the previous story, or a readying statement? For example, in this morning's story, I introduced the disciples and asked listeners to note how Jesus and the disciples treated a specific group. Now it's time to...

If the group is having a difficult time distinguishing between the Opening and the Background, go through the exercise on page 10. After reading the statement, they need to say which one it is.

Tell the Bible Story: It is essential to emphasize to the audience that the story is from the Bible and is accurate. At this point, the audience should keep their Bibles closed and focus completely on the storyteller and the narrative.

Retell: Participants must retell the story for clarification and Biblical accuracy, which can be achieved by everyone re-reading the Bible passage, or the leader asking sequential, Bible-based questions. Games, songs, or art are also effective methods. For "Jesus Blessing the Children," the story was retold with THE ROPE GAME (or different, if you used an alternate activity). These methods will be experienced later.

Discover & Response: Asking questions and discussing the story, is vital for learning. Leaders must shift the focus from merely recalling facts to engaging with God's work in one's heart. The Response should include prayer for the Holy Spirit to speak to each individual. Unlike curriculum with one application point, storying encourages the Spirit to reveal personal implications, often resulting in diverse responses—which is positive. Follow up in subsequent weeks to gauge obedience to God's direction.

SESSION 3: Kid-Centered Teaching & Facilitation

TRAINER NOTE

TRAINER NOTES (Paradigm Shift)

  • The "90% Rule": In a kid-centered environment, the facilitator should strive for the children to be actively involved in 90% of the process.
  • Acquisition vs. Impartation: Explicitly distinguish between Teaching (imparting knowledge) and Training (acquiring a skill). KIDStory is a training methodology focused on results, not just lectures.
  • The "Shift" Challenge: Ask participants to draw a Star next to the role they use most often and a Circle next to the role they need to develop to create a better relational environment.

The Heart of Facilitation: Creating Environments

The goal of a KIDStory leader is not merely to ask "good" questions, but to create a kid-centered environment where children feel free and safe to share. Even a brilliant question will go unanswered if a child does not feel emotionally secure.

Let the children come to Me, and don’t ever stand in their way, for this is what the kingdom of God is all about. Mark 10:14b (The Voice)

Kid-Centered vs. Teacher-Centered:

  • Teacher-Centered: Focuses on the teacher and their ability to impart knowledge.
  • Kid-Centered: Focuses on facilitation where children are active participants in God's Word rather than distant learners.

The 6 Roles of a KIDStory Facilitator

To create a relational environment, you must shift between different roles depending on the needs of the children:

  • Connector: Helping children build bridges with one another and the story.
  • Cheerleader: Celebrating the participation and insights of every child.
  • Encourager: Providing the support needed for kids to take emotional risks.
  • Leader / Guide: Navigating the group through the story's journey.
  • Listener: Attending to the Holy Spirit and the heart of the child.
  • Teacher: Imparting biblical truth through the discovery process.

Keys to Effective Facilitation

Use these "keys" to unlock deeper engagement during your StoryClub:

  • Embrace Wonder & Mystery: Allow room for things we don't fully understand.
  • Safe Space: Ensure everyone has a voice, without fear of embarrassment or shame.
  • Quality Questions: Move from Head (Facts) to Heart (Feelings) to Hand (Action).
  • Be Attentive: Observe body language and tone to stay in tune with the room.
  • Give Time: Silence is your friend; give children ample time to think and share.
  • Redirect: Gently pull the conversation back to the story if it wanders.
  • Encourage: Encouraging participation through positive affirmation and creating on-ramps brings every voice into the circle.

SESSION 4: Learning and Crafting the Story

TRAINER NOTE

TRAINER NOTES (Internalization & Performance)

  • Visual Over Verbal: Use the "Scene Method." Instead of memorizing words, have participants draw nine boxes representing the "chapters" of a story.
  • The Vocal Toolbox: Demonstrate a "monotone" voice versus an "expressive" voice. Ask: "Which one would a 7-year-old listen to?"
  • Feedback Loops: When participants practice telling stories in pairs, give them a specific "Success Criterion," such as: "Did your partner use at least one intentional pause?".

Preparing Your Heart

We should share God's story with enthusiasm, treating it as a dynamic, personal experience rather than a collection of dry facts to be memorized. Our goal is to enable children to truly engage with the narrative, deepen their understanding of God, and confidently share the story with others.

When you return home and tell people about your experience here, are you thinking and memorizing everything you want to say? No! You are remembering people and events that impacted you. Why would it be different with God’s story?

First we need to prepare ourselves…

  • Prayer: Ask the Holy Spirit to show you who you are in the story.
  • Immersion: Read the story multiple times, ideally from different translations. Note what God reveals to you and who you relate to.
  • Spirit-Led Insight: Remember, the Spirit may speak differently to your group than He did to you—remain open to their discovery.
  • Perspectives: Visualize the scene: what are the sensory details and emotional atmosphere? Consider the different characters' and God's perspectives. Answer the basic questions (Who, What, When, Where, Why, How). Place yourself physically in the setting and narrate the story in the third person.

Participant Insight: Think of a story from your childhood. You don't remember the exact words your parents said, but you remember the "pictures" and how it felt. This is how we learn God's Word.

Breaking the Story Down

To make a story memorable, break it into 4 or 5 "Chapters" or “Scenes”. Visualize each scene as if it were a movie in your mind.

The Accuracy Rule: We want the story to be Memorable, but it must remain Biblically Accurate, because "creative" does not mean "changing the facts". We never add our own interpretations or details that aren't in the Word.

Peer-to-Peer Recap: In pairs, take 3 minutes to read the story of Jesus and Peter Walking on the Water (Matthew 14). Identify the 4 main scenes. Compare your scenes with your partner's.

Tools for Learning the Story

There is no "right" way to learn. Choose the method that fits your learning style:

  • Read & Tell: Read it, close the Bible, and tell it out loud.
  • Mindmaps: Use a central idea and branches for details.
  • Storyboarding: Sketch the main events in simple boxes.
  • Room Locations: Move to different parts of the room for each new scene.
  • Hand Gestures: Create a motion for every "key" action.
  • Key Words: Reiterate important words or phrases, having the children repeat them, or fill them in.

Crafting for Your Audience

Crafting is the skill of making the story understandable without losing its worth.

  • Assumed Knowledge: Biblical authors wrote assuming listeners understood their culture (e.g., the Good Samaritan parable and the Jewish dislike of Samaritans). This necessary cultural context should be included in the Background, supported by other scripture references if possible.
  • Age Appropriateness: Swap difficult words for synonyms a child understands while keeping the biblical meaning.
  • Remove "The Bogs": Some lists of names or specific locations distract children from the main point; it’s okay to simplify these for clarity.
  • Bridge the Gaps: If the story relies on context from previous stories, explain this briefly in the Background.
  • Cultural Offenses: Be sensitive to things in the story that may offend participants (Jesus being alone with the woman at the well.)
ACTIVITY

SESSION 5: Tell the Story (Delivery & Engagement)

TRAINER NOTE

TRAINER NOTES (Sensory & Vocal Facilitation)

  • The "Experience" Factor: Everyone retains more when they do something rather than just hear it. Instead of lecturing on the five senses, have groups "blind-test" an object to guess how it relates to the story of Peter walking on the water.
  • Vocal Variety Exercise: Demonstrate a "monotone" voice versus an "expressive" voice. Ask the participants: "Which one makes you want to lean in, and which one makes you want to look at your phone?"
  • The Power of the Pause: Trainers often rush to fill silence. Model the "8-second pause" after a dramatic moment in the story. Explain that this silence is where the Holy Spirit works on the heart.
  • Believability over Performance: Remind participants that they don't need to be professional actors. If they believe the story is true and they are "living it out" with the children, the audience will follow.

God’s Style of Storytelling

We aim to tell stories in a way that is Memorable, Unexpected, and Multi-Sensory. God used these same principles throughout Scripture to ensure His message was never forgotten. Think about how you might use your location, lighting, seating, or even their own senses to surprise the audience and raise curiosity.

  • Place: Sitting in small groups (Jesus Feeds the Multitudes), or like they're in a boat (Jesus Walks on the Water)
  • Visual: Costumes or simple objects like a piece of fabric.
  • Touch: Passing an object or a light splash of water.
  • Hear: Rubbing hands for rain or vocal sound effects.
  • Smell/Taste: Incense, fish, or even a piece of bread.

Above All … Live it Out

"We cared so much for you that we were pleased to share with you not only the Gospel of God, but also our own lives..." — 1 Thessalonians 2:8.

Your passion and presentation for the story is not what the children will remember most.

Remember the "Live It Out" principle. Our individual actions and attitudes often help children understand who Jesus is more than our spoken words.

Preach the Gospel at all times … and words are helpful too.

The Storyteller’s Toolbox: Delivery

Your voice and body are the primary instruments for discipling the children's imagination.

  • Movement: Do not stay glued to your chair. Move through the "locations" of the story to help the children visualize the journey.
  • Voice (Pitch, Pace, and Pause):
  • Vary your Pitch to represent different characters or emotions.
  • Control your Pace—slow down for mystery and speed up for action.
  • Utilize the Pause to let a specific predicament or truth sink from the head to the heart.
  • Facial Expressions: Your expressions make the story believable. If your face doesn't reflect the emotion of the text, the children will sense a disconnect.

Peer-to-Peer Recap: The Expression Challenge Turn to a partner. Without using words, use your face to portray 3 emotions from the Expressions Sheet (e.g., frightened, amazed, frustrated). Your partner must guess the emotion and identify a moment in the "Jesus and Peter" story where a character felt that way.

Audience Participation

Get the children into the story, not just in front of it.

  • Reoccurring Phrases: Let the group join in on repeated words.
  • Hand & Body Motions: Assign motions for specific actions (e.g., rowing a boat, blowing wind, etc.).
  • Human Slide Show: Have children freeze in "poses" representing scenes as you tell them.

SESSION 6: Retell the Story

TRAINER NOTE

TRAINER NOTES (Pedagogy & Engagement)

  • The 90/20/8 Rule: This section is the "activity" peak of your workshop. After 20 minutes of "Telling," participants need a change of pace. Use these games to reset their energy.
  • Connection Before Content: Before showing the games, ask participants: "What was your favorite childhood game?" Connect that feeling of joy to the "Universals" of play and belonging.
  • Peer-to-Peer Accountability: Don't just explain the games; have groups teach a game to another group. This builds "Reproducing" skills—a core KIDStory essential.
  • The Goal is Accuracy: Always remind participants that games aren't just for fun; they are a "Clarification Tool" to ensure the story remains Biblically accurate.

Why We Retell

Retelling is the bridge between hearing and owning the Word. It provides clarification and ensures Biblical accuracy. When children (and adults) retell the story, they transition from being listeners to being messengers.

We utilize The Universals—common elements found in every culture—to make retelling natural and engaging:

  • Belonging/Family
  • Music
  • Games/Fun
  • Arts (Drama, Dance, Visual Arts)
  • Stories

Retell Methods: (see full list in back)

Games help children focus and provide a low-pressure way to repeat the story details. For children with different "Intelligences" (Nature, Logic, or Self-Smart), more artistic methods may allow for deeper reflection.

Drama

  • Circle Toss: Toss a ball or soft object. The person who catches it tells the next part of the Bible story. A balloon can also be used.
  • Knot You: Tie a rope into a circle. Have kids hold the rope. On your command, they move it clockwise. When you say "Stop," the child holding the knot answers a question about the Bible story or tells what happens next.
  • Musical Chairs: Arrange chairs in a circle, one less than the group size. As music plays, people walk around. When the music stops, everyone finds a chair. The person left standing tells the next part of the story. Repeat until the story is complete.

Drama

  • Act-it-Out: Assign different parts of the story to children in the group. Have them practice and then act out the story to the rest of the group.
  • Object(s) (ie: scarf, rope): See how many times they can use the object to tell the story. You can also give each group 3 different random objects to use in retelling the story.
  • Motions: Illustrate each story segment with a distinct motion. Assign a motion to each child or small group to perform sequentially.

Music

  • Rap Review: Have the kids create a rap to the main events of the story.
  • New Lyrics: Have the children pick a simple children’s song and change the words so the Bible story is being told through a song.
  • Dance: create a dance that would tell the story. Put some background music to it.

Writing

  • Storybooks: the story is retold by the child through pictures and/or words in a storybook (or comic) format
  • Top Ten: have the kids, either as a group or individually, write out the top ten events of the story.
  • What’s Next: Have each child write out what God wants them to do with what they heard in the story

SESSION 7: Discover & Response Questions

TRAINER NOTE

TRAINER NOTES (The Art of the Question)

  • The 80/20 Rule of Facilitation: Aim for the participants to speak 80% of the time while the leader speaks only 20%. The leader's role is to facilitate discovery, not to lecture.
  • "Don't Steal the 'Aha!' Moment": When a leader gives the answer, the dialogue dies. If a participant asks a question, "push it back" to the group to allow them to think and gain insight.
  • The Ladder of Inference: Use the Head Heart Hands progression. We start with facts (Head) to build a foundation, move to feelings (Heart) to create connection, and end with application (Hands) to spark obedience.
  • Wait Time: After asking a question, embrace 8–10 seconds of silence. Silence allows the Holy Spirit to move the truth from the head to the heart.

The Power of the Question

Did You Know: Jesus asked nearly 100 questions for every ONE HE ANSWERED!

Engaging in questions and the subsequent discussion is vital to the storying process. Simply knowing the facts is secondary. A leader should guide the group beyond merely remembering story facts to exploring the story. This creates space for the Holy Spirit to work, transforming lives so people become more like Jesus Christ. Jesus consistently used questions to lead people to deeper understanding and heart transformation. Facilitating discussions helps children discover the treasures in the Bible, allowing the Scripture to penetrate their hearts.

Asking questions leads people to discover God's truths for themselves, internalizing them deeper than simply being told. It’s not about what YOU know – it’s about THEM discovering God’s truths for themselves! DISCOVER QUESTIONS build an interactive discussion around these areas:

1. Discover Questions: The HEAD (Facts)

Goal: To ensure full comprehension, the goal is for the child to articulate and recall the story's sequence of events and main elements. This requires grasping the narrative's core: the key characters, the conflict, the setting, and the resolution. This foundational understanding is necessary before deeper analysis.

  • What happened in the story?
  • What did you notice or what stood out to you?
  • What choices did the people make?
  • What does this story tell us about God or Jesus?

2. Discover Questions: The HEART (Feelings)

Goal: To truly connect, we must answer the child's "why" from the heart, establishing relationships that go beyond mere information transfer. Most curricula prioritize knowledge, allowing children to default to expected answers ("God," "Jesus," etc.) without engaging their minds or emotions. This lack of deep, personal connection likely contributes to the decline in personal relationships with Jesus as children age.

  • What feelings did the people in the story experience?
  • Which character do you most relate to?
  • I wonder why God put this story in the Bible?
  • How did this story challenge or encourage you?

3. Response Questions: The HANDS (Action)

Goal: This step is an intentional invitation for the Holy Spirit to guide how the Scripture applies to one's life. Instead of relying only on human understanding, participants humbly ask for God's help to see how the story's truths and examples should practically change their daily choices, relationships, and views. It is the necessary move from understanding to Spirit-led action.

  • What do you feel the Holy Spirit is asking you to do because of this story?
  • What should you do differently this week?
  • Who can you share this story with this week?

Possible Core Questions

  • How did the people in the story trust and follow God? or How did they not obey God?
  • What feelings did the people in our story experience? Have you felt that way?
  • If I could tell God something, I would want Him to know that ______________.
  • What does this story tell us about God / Jesus / Holy Spirit?
  • After listening to today’s Bible Story, God wants me to _______________. What is the Holy Spirit speaking to you from our time together today?
  • Who can you share this story with this week?

Handling Tangents, Difficult Moments, or Specific Topics

  • Keep it Focused: If children get off-topic, gently push them back to the story by asking, "Where do you see that in today's story?".
  • The "I Don't Know" Policy: If you are asked a difficult theological question, it is okay to say, "That is a great question. I need to go home and find the answer for you".
  • No One "Right" Answer: Except for basic facts, avoid looking for a single "correct" response. Allow multiple people to respond to open-ended questions to encourage a diversity of perspectives.
  • Topical Guardrails: When using the Storying method with a Bible story to address a specific topic or concern, guide questions toward the desired area without forcing specific answers.

This Isn’t 20 Questions

  • Move Beyond Facts: Except for initial framing questions, avoid seeking a single right answer.
  • Create Dialogue: After open-ended questions, allow multiple responses, as learning and revelation come through everyone's input.
  • Go Deeper: Follow up on initial responses with probing questions to reveal more, such as "What made you feel or think that way?" or "Why is that important to you?" Maintain a safe environment for sharing.
  • Let Them Work Through It: When asked a question, leaders should push it back to the group for discussion. Giving the first response often ends the dialogue; pushing it back promotes group thought and deeper insight.

SESSION 8: Final Assignment & Lesson Planning

TRAINER NOTE

TRAINER NOTES (The Practicum)

  • Instructor-Led, Participant-Centered: Shift your role from "Teacher" to "Consultant". Walk between the groups as they plan, offering guidance only when they are stuck.
  • The "Safe Fail" Environment: Encourage participants to take risks with their "Tell" and "Retell" sections. Remind them that the workshop is a laboratory for experimentation.
  • Connection through Collaboration: Assigning roles within the small groups (e.g., Storyteller, Questioner, Prop Master) ensures that every participant has a "job" and stays engaged.
  • Accuracy is the Anchor: While you want participants to be creative, your primary feedback should focus on Biblical Accuracy. If a group "embellishes" the story with details not found in the Word, gently guide them back to the text.

SESSION 9: Closing & Vision Casting

TRAINER NOTE

TRAINER NOTES (Made to Multiply)

  • The "Exponential" Mindset: Most leaders are trained in addition (filling a classroom). Challenge them to think in multiplication (reproducing the training). Use the math of 1x3 to show how the Great Commission actually works.
  • Connection to the "Why”: Ask: "Who is the person who first told you about Jesus?" Remind them that they are a link in a chain that stretches back to the first 12 disciples.
  • The Apprentice Strategy: A leader’s success isn’t measured by how many children attend, but by how many leaders they leave behind. Encourage them to name their "Apprentice" before they leave the room today.
  • Active Visioning: Have participants close their eyes and move from "imagining a room" to "imagining a movement" that reaches the whole community.

The Call to Multiplication

Jesus did not just give us a message; He gave us a method. He poured into 12 men and commissioned them to do the same. Every Christian today can trace their spiritual lineage back to that commitment to Multiply, not just Add.

Addition vs. Multiplication:

  • Addition: One leader adds one other at a time (1 + 1 + 1 ...). Growth is slow and limited by the leader's energy.
  • Multiplication: One leader trains three others, who each train three more (1 x 3 3 x 3 9 x 3 = 27 … 81, 243, 729, 2187, 6561, 19683, 59049, 177147, 531441, 1594323). The growth explodes, reaching children and communities that one person could never reach alone.

Starting Your StoryClub

A StoryClub is a relational environment where children encounter Jesus. Whether you meet under a tree, in a home, or in a church, your goal is to follow through on what God is calling you to do with what you have learned.

  • Implement Immediately: Don't wait for the "perfect" setup. Start using these methods in your current discipleship environments next week.
  • Identify Your Apprentice: Look for someone the Lord is asking you to invite to join and apprentice with you. Multiplication starts by sharing your "seat" at the table.
  • Empower the Children: Remember that children can retell these stories to family and friends immediately. They are your primary "Fishers of Men".

PARTICIPANT EXERCISE: "The Non-Dominant Hand Challenge"

  • The Task: Ask participants to open the inside cover of their training manual. Instruct them to write their full name using their non-dominant hand. Encourage them to take their time and focus.
  • The Check-in: Once everyone is finished, ask the following questions:
  • How did that feel? (Wait for responses like awkward, challenging, strange, difficult, slow).
  • Do you plan to start writing with your non-dominant hand from now on? (The answer should be a clear 'No').
  • The Application: Explain to the group that the feeling they just experienced is exactly how the KIDStory methods might feel when they first try to implement them back home.
  • The Awkwardness: Moving from lecturing (what they are dominant at) to facilitating (the non-dominant, new skill) will feel strange.
  • The Commitment: The answer to whether you will continue using your non-dominant hand is "no," because you don't have to. But what if you lost the use of your dominant hand? You would learn to write again because it matters.
  • The Vision: We push through the initial awkwardness of a new method—the slow process of listening and asking questions—because nothing matters more than introducing a child to Jesus. If it matters, we will learn how to do it. You have the tools; now you must choose to practice.
